Start here

The secret to a great master is a great mix.

If Jay-J needs drastic EQ to make a master work, he goes back to the mix. When a client's kick is fighting everything, he calls them and asks them to fix the kick. They send a better mix, he sends back a better master.

Mastering is not where you fix the song. It is where you live in the subtleties: barely any EQ, barely any dynamics, and a loud, balanced, polished version of a song that already works.

Listening test  ·  mix vs. master, same loudness

What mastering actually changed

Darker mix, lifted top

Less energy below 300 Hz, more from 3 to 8 kHz. The synth and hats come forward. Drum attacks get a little rounder.

Bass tucked, presence up

The bass is held back, which actually gives it more room. The vocal, claps and percussion get presence.

Warmer mids

The shift sits between 300 Hz and 1 kHz: warmer vocal, softer top. The hardest one to hear, because our ears are most sensitive up around 4 kHz.

Go A, then B, then back to A. A suddenly sounds dull, even though nothing about it changed. Your perspective did.

Curves are illustrative shapes, not measurements

Why you could hear it

You became a better mixer because you got better perspective.

No new EQ. No better speakers. No years of ear training. Every pair was level matched, so loudness could not fool anyone, and new ears still heard the difference.

Louder always sounds better. Put your rough mix next to a mastered record and you think “I suck at this.” Level match them and you hear “oh, I just have too much bass.”

Step one

Export the mix. Change nothing.

  • Sample rateWhatever you worked at. 44.1 in, 44.1 out. 48 in, 48 out.
  • File typeWAV or AIFF, same as the session.
  • Bit depthSame or higher, never lower. Live defaults to 32-bit float, so export 32.
  • DitherOff.
  • Limiter, bus compOff. Bus compression becomes one of the first steps of the master.
  • HeadroomAbout 6 dB. If that means pulling the master fader down, fine. Your DAW's math has you covered.

Any change to sample rate, bit depth or file type happens once, at the very end of mastering.

Wear one hat at a time

A mix session, then a master session

Why keep them apart One job per session In one big session you start mastering, hear the kick, tweak an EQ, swap the bass sound, and forget you were mastering. Jay-J's advice: keep them separate for your next seven to ten years of making music.
Before you master Take a day Car, headphones, phone speaker. Does the arrangement still hit? Anything clipping? Are you actually ready to master it?

Hear the master and the kick sucks? Great. Open the mix, fix the kick, re-export. No big deal.

Step two  ·  the master session

Two listening paths, one set of meters

Your processing lives on the track. The Master holds nothing but meters, so your song and your references get measured by the same tools at the same level. Solo one or the other, never both.

Jay-J's weird trick

Everything you compare sits at -10 LUFS

References-10 LUFSClip gain every reference to one round number.
Your master-7 LUFSHis usual target for house. Yours might be -5 or -8.
Group fader-3 dBThe gap between them. Audition only, back to 0 for export.
Your target (LUFS)-8-7-6-5-4
Group fader (dB)-2-3-4-5-6

The number matters less than the consistency. Pick one reference level and keep it. If a reference measures quieter than -10, do not boost it. Bring the whole set down to match the quietest one.

Step three

Level match your references. One minute each.

  1. 01Pick four to seven records you love. Not one. One reference is too narrow. A group lets you hear the middle ground.
  2. 02Cut a 30-second clip of the main section. The drop, the hook, the part that matters.
  3. 03Play it once through a loudness meter (Youlean is free) and read the integrated LUFS.
  4. 04Clip gain the difference. Reads -6.6? Clip gain -3.4 dB.
  5. 05Play it again. It settles at -10. Next reference.

Why 10? Because “minus math is hard enough.”

The part people skip

Use the references on your mix first

Drop the same clips into your mix session. They all sit at -10, so one fader moves the whole set down to wherever your mix happens to be.

  1. 01Loop the main section of your song and flip between it and the references.
  2. 02Write down what you hear. Hats too loud. Sub too loud. Vocal buried.
  3. 03Fix it in the mix and export. Then bring the same reference clips straight into the master session.

A better mix makes mastering easy.

Listening at matched level

Four things to listen for

01 · Dynamics Round or sharp? Listen only to the front of every sound, not the tail. How do the attacks feel? What happens between hits?
02 · Tone How do lows, mids and highs relate? Is the problem the whole mix, or one sound?
03 · Color Clean or gritty? Warm, bright, dense, smooth. Which one suits this song?
04 · Stereo image What is in the middle? What is wide? Does the width add impact or blur it?

It is also the order Jay-J works in: dynamics, tone, color, width.

Can't hear some of this yet? Normal. Deciding what to listen for is how you learn to hear it.

Chain  ·  02

Center the low end first

Utility's Bass Mono at 120 Hz, first in the chain. A safety net that will not hurt anything.

Why

Big-system subwoofers only handle mono. Feed them only mono and you have your best shot at translating on a club rig, and on a small speaker too.

In the mix too

Jay-J uses bass mono all the time on kicks and basses. Stereo in the bass, stereo in the kick, stereo in everything. Except the low end.

Chain  ·  03  ·  bus compression

Give the record a gentle hug

Ableton's Glue Compressor, as Jay-J set it.

Sidechain EQHigh-pass ~276 Hz
Attack10 ms
Release0.2 s
Ratio2:1 to 4:1
Gain reductionAbout 1 to 3 dB
No sidechain EQ?Attack 30 ms

The sidechain EQ changes what the compressor listens to, not what you hear. It ignores the sub, reacts to the mids and highs, and still compresses the whole mix.

How to hear it

Your claps and shakers stop sounding like individuals.

Ignore everything except the transients in the mids and highs: shakers, claps, percussion, ear candy. Before, each one hits on its own. After, they move as a group. That is the hug.

If the whole mix dips every time the kick or bass hits, raise the sidechain filter or back off the threshold.

Chain  ·  04  ·  parallel compression

Then lift it from below

TDR Kotelnikov. Free, and truly parallel.

Threshold-50 dB
Peak crestPeak
Soft knee0 dB
Ratio2.5:1
Attack / release0.02 ms / 275 ms
Makeup-10 dB
Dry mix0 dB

The dry signal passes at full level and the squashed copy sits 10 dB underneath. Bob Katz's move. The hug works from the top, this works from the bottom, lifting everything between the transients.

Chain  ·  11, set early

Get to level first. Then shape the tone.

Jay-J sets the limiter right after the compression, before any EQ. Limiting changes the balance, bringing up sub and mid-range. Get loud first and you only EQ once.

  1. 01The EQ still goes before the limiter in the chain. You just set the limiter first.
  2. 02Watch the loudness meter on the Master. With the group at -3 dB you are aiming for -10 LUFS there, which is -7 at the output.
  3. 03Then cycle. Too much bass next to the references? Pull it down, readjust the limiter, compare again.

Keep the ceiling around -1 dB so the export has room for true peaks.

He pulled Range Hi down until his reference kicks just touched the top, and Range Lo up until 30 Hz sat on the floor. Now your kick under the top? Bring it up. Over the top? Bring it down.

Seeing it  ·  Tonal Balance Control

Until you level match, a spectrum analyzer is useless.

An analyzer shows level at each frequency. If one track is louder, every frequency looks louder, and you learn nothing. SPAN has been around since the early 2000s. Jay-J only started using it seriously about seven years ago, once level matching made it click.

Tonal Balance Control is a spectrum analyzer with the level matching built in. Compare the shape of your track to the shape your references make.

How Jay-J actually works

The night-before trick

  1. 01The night before, open the master. Look at SPAN and Tonal Balance.
  2. 02Make one quick EQ move on the low end. Save. Close it. Go to bed.
  3. 03Next day, spend your fresh ears on the mids and highs. The low end is already in the ballpark, because you know where your references hit.

“I'm lazy, but also I have a high standard.”

Every odd trick in this workflow exists to hit a high bar in less time.

Chain  ·  05, 07, 08

Only if the song asks for it

Width

A multiband imager (Ozone's) can widen the mids and highs or tighten the low mids. The free single-band one is fine on a track, not across a whole master. Can't hear width yet? Skip it.

Multiband

One range gets aggressive in one section, and a band catches just that. Free option: TDR Nova. The better fix is usually the mix: turn down that lead in the third drop.

Color

Saturation, Roar, an exciter. Keep it subtle. Once you notice it, it is usually too much, because it is distortion. Making dubstep? Go nuts.

Each stage does a little. If a stage does nothing for this song, leave it out.

How to work

One goal at a time

  1. 01Play your song and the references. Pick one goal. “The vocal needs to come out.”
  2. 02Try one thing. Some EQ, some dynamics, maybe pulling the top end down.
  3. 03Bypass it. On, off, on. Then back to the references.
  4. 04Keep it if it fixed the goal at matched level, not because it got louder.
  5. 05Next goal. “My low end isn't powerful enough.” A little more compression? A Pultec-style lift?

Finish

Export the master, then walk away

Before you bounce
  • Group fader back to 0 dB. Now the output hits your target.
  • Turn off every plug-in on the Master. Nothing after the limiter.
  • Mute the references.
  • Room or headphone correction off, if you use it.
Then fresh ears
  • Give it a day.
  • Car, different headphones, a friend you trust.
  • Something wrong? Go back as far as you need: fix the mix, re-export, re-level-match, tweak the chain.

The audition setup and the delivery are two different jobs. Only the second one gets exported.

If you remember one thing

Analytics, level matched.

That alone levels you up. Better perspective makes you a better mixer, and the rest is minutiae.
